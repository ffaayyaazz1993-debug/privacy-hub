Smart Privacy Screen

A Michael Porter–style read on the shoulder-surfing / screen-privacy app market.
Framework: Porter’s Five Forces · Generic Strategies · Strategic Groups  ·  Grounded in live Play Store competitors  ·  Nov 2026

Define the business by the need, not the product. You’re not selling “a blackout overlay” — you’re selling protection from shoulder-surfing in public. The need is real, but software is physically limited (it dims for everyone; it can’t narrow viewing angles). That single fact drives every force below.

01Five Forces — is this an attractive market?

Porter’s test of segment attractiveness. High forces = thin, fragile margins.

Threat of new entrants

A simple overlay, no capital, no proprietary IP, free Play Store distribution. A working clone can be built in days — the store is already full of near-identical “Screen Guard / Privacy Filter” apps.

High

Rivalry among existing competitors

Dozens of undifferentiated apps (Screen Guard, Redact, Privacy Display, Tilt Veil, VEIL…). They compete on keyword-stuffed listings and free/freemium pricing, not capability. Zero switching cost → a price-and-ASO war.

High

Threat of substitutes — the big one

Hardware micro-louver protectors (3M, StarTech, ~$10–40) actually block side-view light; software can’t. OEM built-ins (Samsung S25 Privacy Display, HP Sure View, Dell SafeScreen) narrow angles electronically. Even lowering brightness substitutes. For the core optical job, substitutes are genuinely better.

High

Bargaining power of buyers

Many free alternatives, no lock-in, no switching cost, low involvement. Users install, try once, churn. Buyers hold all the power.

High

Bargaining power of suppliers

Concentrated in one “supplier”: the platform. Google Play takes 15–30%, sets policy, can delist you; Android controls the overlay/accessibility permissions the whole category depends on — one OS change can break the product. Dev tools/talent are low-power.

Mod–High
Verdict: structurally unattractive — a classic “red ocean.” High entry + high rivalry + strong substitutes + buyer power = thin, fragile margins. Enter only with eyes open, and never on features or price alone.

03Generic strategies — where can you win?

Porter’s three routes to advantage, tested against this market.
No

Cost leadership

You can’t undercut free, and it needs scale you don’t have.

Weak

Broad differentiation

Features are copyable in a weekend; VEIL already matches the window. No durable feature gap.

Viable

Focus (niche)

The only viable play. Pick a segment/use-case the generic dimmers serve badly and own it.

04Is your advantage defensible? — the moat test

  • Real engineering edge: the uncovered-aperture design gives genuine system-wide touch passthrough with no accessibility — many rivals’ full-screen overlays block touch or lean on accessibility. Real, but replicable and invisible to users. Not a moat.
  • Trust edge: the category is full of misleading “blocks side viewing” claims; the honest “software privacy utility, not optical protection” stance stands out. But honesty alone doesn’t convert installs.
  • Missing: no network effects, no switching costs, no scale economies, no brand yet. Moat = weak.

05Recommended positioning

What the forces imply you should actually do.
  1. Don’t fight the commodity dimmer war. Differentiate on the one thing hardware can’t do: dynamic, selective, reversible privacy. A hardware filter is always-on and dims everything; your software can reveal a window, auto-trigger, and scope to specific apps.
  2. Position as a complement to a hardware protector, not a replacement — the “hybrid” approach security writers recommend. It’s honest and sidesteps the unwinnable optical claim.
  3. Own a sharp use-case: “read / bank privately in public over ANY app, with real touch” — commuters, open-plan offices, travelers. Narrow the focus until you’re the obvious choice for it.
  4. Compete on trust + UX: honest claims, no data collection (like Redact), smooth drag/resize, real passthrough. These are review-worthy in a spammy category.
  5. Monetize freemium like VEIL: free dim/blackout; paid window + automation + per-app rules.
  6. Avoid the “blocks side view” claim — the category’s biggest credibility / legal / review trap, and it contradicts the honest brand.
Bottom line: an unattractive, crowded market with strong hardware substitutes. You won’t win on features or price. The defensible path is a focused niche + trustworthy branding + the dynamic/selective privacy hardware can’t match — sold as a complement to a physical filter, not a substitute for it.
